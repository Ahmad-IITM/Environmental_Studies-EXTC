Unit 04 · Variation and resilience

Biodiversity and its Conservation

Biodiversity is the variety inside a species, between species and across ecosystems.

On this pageSummaryVisualCase Study (India)The EXTC LinkAI-use note

Summary

Biodiversity has three connected levels. Genetic diversity is variation within a species, such as different crop varieties or adaptations in a wild population. Species diversity concerns the number and relative abundance of species. Ecosystem diversity includes forests, grasslands, wetlands, deserts, coral reefs, rivers and the processes that connect them. Losing one level can weaken the others: a small isolated population may lose genetic variation, while the loss of a habitat removes many species together.

India is recognised as a megadiverse country because it contains high biological diversity in a comparatively small share of the world's land area, including many endemic species. Mountains, coasts, islands, tropical forests, dry regions and rivers create varied habitats. Four global biodiversity hotspots overlap parts of India: the Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka and Sundaland, with the Nicobar Islands associated with the last region. A hotspot is not simply a beautiful place; it is a region with exceptional endemic species and substantial habitat loss.

Threats include habitat destruction and fragmentation, over-exploitation, invasive alien species, pollution, disease and climate change. Roads, farms, mines and cities can split a habitat into small patches. Illegal wildlife trade and unsustainable harvest remove individuals faster than populations recover. Climate change shifts temperature and rainfall, making it difficult for species to move or adapt. These pressures often combine, so a single protected area cannot solve every problem.

In-situ conservation protects species within their natural habitat through national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, community reserves and habitat corridors. Ex-situ conservation protects genetic material or living organisms outside the habitat through seed banks, botanical gardens, zoos, aquaria and captive breeding. Ex-situ methods are valuable backups, but they cannot recreate every ecological interaction. Conservation also requires local participation, enforcement, scientific monitoring and fair livelihoods.

We can think of biodiversity as a portfolio of options for an uncertain future. Genetic and ecosystem variety can make a landscape more resilient, but only if connections and ecological processes remain. An engineer designing a camera trap, acoustic monitor or satellite map must therefore measure with care and avoid turning conservation into surveillance without context.

That is why environmental study is also a habit of listening: measurements, local experience and institutional responsibility must inform one another before a decision is made.

Another useful distinction is between a problem, an indicator and an intervention. A falling water level is an indicator; the causes may include rainfall, pumping and land-use change; the intervention might be recharge, pricing, leakage control or a different crop. Asking these questions prevents us from treating a visible symptom as the whole environmental story. It also keeps public awareness practical, because people can see what information supports which choice.

This perspective is useful in an exam as well as in the field. We can move from a definition to a relationship, from a relationship to an Indian example, and from that example to a responsible intervention. The environment is not background scenery around human activity; it is the set of conditions that makes activity possible and sets its limits.

Case Study (India): Western Ghats

Background: The Western Ghats run along India's western side and include forests, rivers, farms and settlements. Their varied elevation and rainfall support many endemic plants and animals and feed important peninsular rivers.

What happened: Habitat fragmentation, roads, mining, plantations, dams, urban growth and climate pressures have placed stress on species and corridors. The region has also experienced intense debate over how conservation boundaries should affect residents and livelihoods.

Impact and response: Protected areas, wildlife corridors, restoration, environmental assessment and community conservation are used in different places. Scientific panels and public consultations have repeatedly highlighted the need to combine ecological sensitivity with practical governance.

Lesson: A hotspot is a living landscape, not a museum. Conservation succeeds when it protects habitat and respects people while using evidence to guide where development can occur.

The EXTC Link

Electronics can extend the eyes and ears of conservation teams. Camera traps use passive infrared sensing, a lens and an embedded controller to record animals at night. Acoustic sensors can monitor birds, frogs, bats or illegal chainsaw activity. A Raspberry Pi or low-power ARM board can run a compact classifier, but training data must represent seasons, locations and background sounds. Otherwise, an algorithm may identify the recording conditions rather than the species.

Remote sensing adds landscape scale. Satellite imagery can help map forest cover, fragmentation, water and fire scars. ISRO and NRSC platforms, including Bhuvan services, can support Indian geospatial analysis, but maps need ground truth and careful classification. A change-detection pipeline should report cloud cover, spatial resolution and false positives. GPS tags can study movement, yet their use needs ethical review and animal-welfare safeguards.

A field node might combine a PIR camera, microphone, temperature-humidity sensor and solar supply. LoRaWAN sends event metadata where gateways exist; 4G or NB-IoT can carry thumbnails and alerts; store-and-forward handles outages. Filtering removes wind rumble, and a spectrogram makes calls easier to compare. A tamper switch can report theft, while secure identity prevents a fake reading from entering the database.

The system should minimise collection. Send “probable elephant detected” and a confidence score when possible, rather than a constant stream of private or sensitive imagery. Conservation staff need a map of effort and missing data as much as a map of detections. Technology supports biodiversity when its design respects both living subjects and local governance.

Proposed monitoring combines local detection with landscape context and human review.

In practice, we would document assumptions, test failure modes and make the uncertainty visible to the person who has to act on the result. A maintainable prototype is more valuable than an impressive demonstration that cannot survive field conditions.

We would also plan ownership before deployment: who receives the alert, who can repair the node, who stores the data and who reviews a doubtful reading. A field protocol would include a reference measurement, a maintenance schedule and a way to report damage. These details connect embedded systems to environmental governance. Without them, a technically correct circuit can still produce an unreliable public service.

Finally, a deployment should have a human fallback. If the radio is unavailable, if the sensor drifts or if a model is uncertain, staff need a safe manual procedure. We would log these failures and use them to improve placement, thresholds and training. That feedback loop is the difference between a prototype and an accountable environmental system.
