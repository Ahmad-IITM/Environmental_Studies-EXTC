Looking back at the work

What changed in our thinking?

At the beginning of this assignment, we tended to separate environmental science from engineering. The units made that separation difficult to defend. A forest is not only a biological community; it is also a water regulator, a carbon store, a livelihood and a place where a poorly planned road can change risk. Pollution is not only a chemistry problem. It is a measurement problem, a public-health problem and a communication problem.

We learned to follow a system rather than memorise isolated terms. In the ecosystem unit, energy flow connected producers, consumers and decomposers. In the pollution unit, the source–pathway–receptor model helped us ask who is exposed and what intervention is possible. Indian examples made the theory less abstract: Chipko connected forests with local agency, the Sundarbans showed how habitat and disaster protection overlap, and flood warnings showed why minutes of reliable communication matter.

Our EXTC background changed the questions we asked. We started checking sensor range, calibration drift, sampling rate, packet loss, battery life and what happens when a device fails outdoors. A dashboard can look precise while the underlying sensor is dirty or badly placed. We also understood that technology cannot replace participation. A LoRa node may report a water level, but residents and local authorities still decide what action is fair and practical.

Our roles shaped the work. Ahmad Fansofkar led the team and AI tools; Shreeraj Gaikwad led content for Units I to III; Saurabh Bambade led research for Units IV to VI; Atharv Hatankar led design and QA; and Rushan Godad coordinated fact checking and the verification log. For example, Shreeraj reviewed Units I to III while Saurabh reviewed Units IV to VI, and Rushan checked the claims they flagged.

AI helped us brainstorm structures, compare explanations, draft SVG diagrams and find questions that needed checking. It also produced confident-sounding details that required correction or softening. We treated it as a writing and research assistant, not as a source. We checked laws and institutional claims against official pages, removed unsupported numbers, and kept uncertainty visible where our evidence was limited.

One correction was keeping the Delhi pollution discussion seasonal and multi-source instead of assigning every episode to crop burning. The class discussion about local water and waste made the difference between a technical measurement and a usable public decision clearer to us.

The most useful lesson is that responsible engineering begins before the circuit is built. We need to define the environmental decision, the people affected, the quality of evidence needed and the limits of the system. That habit will matter in telecom networks, embedded products and every future project where a technical choice touches a living place.
