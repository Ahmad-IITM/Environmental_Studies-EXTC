Unit 03 · Flow and feedback

Ecosystems

An ecosystem is less like a box and more like a running conversation between energy, matter and organisms.

On this pageSummaryVisualCase Study (India)The EXTC LinkAI-use note

Summary

An ecosystem includes a community of organisms and the non-living environment with which they interact. Its structure has abiotic factors such as light, temperature, water, soil and nutrients, and biotic components such as producers, consumers and decomposers. Producers, mainly plants and algae, capture solar energy. Consumers obtain energy by feeding on plants or other animals. Decomposers break down dead material and return nutrients to the system.

Energy flows in one direction, while matter cycles. A food chain shows a simple feeding path, such as grass to grasshopper to frog to snake. A food web is more realistic because organisms have several food relationships. If one species declines, the effect can travel through many links. Ecological pyramids represent numbers, biomass or energy at different trophic levels. The energy pyramid is always upright because some energy is lost as heat and used for life processes at every transfer; the often-used ten percent idea is a rough teaching rule, not a universal constant.

Productivity describes the rate at which biomass is produced. Decomposers are not an afterthought: without fungi, bacteria and soil organisms, nutrients would remain locked in dead matter. Ecosystem functions include nutrient cycling, soil formation, pollination, water regulation, carbon storage and habitat provision. A forest, grassland, desert and aquatic ecosystem differ in climate, structure and dominant adaptations, but each has interactions and flows that can be studied.

Succession is the gradual change in community composition over time. Primary succession begins where soil is absent, while secondary succession follows disturbance where soil or organisms remain. It is not always a simple march toward one fixed climax. Fire, grazing, floods, invasive species and human management can redirect the path. A lake can also change as sediment and nutrients accumulate, a process called eutrophication when excess nutrients trigger algal growth and oxygen depletion.

We can picture an ecosystem as a network with both bandwidth and feedback. Remove a pollinator, alter a river flow or add heat, and the network may still function for a while before a threshold is crossed. Ecological thinking helps engineers ask not only whether a system works today, but whether its feedbacks keep it stable tomorrow.

That is why environmental study is also a habit of listening: measurements, local experience and institutional responsibility must inform one another before a decision is made.

Another useful distinction is between a problem, an indicator and an intervention. A falling water level is an indicator; the causes may include rainfall, pumping and land-use change; the intervention might be recharge, pricing, leakage control or a different crop. Asking these questions prevents us from treating a visible symptom as the whole environmental story. It also keeps public awareness practical, because people can see what information supports which choice.

This perspective is useful in an exam as well as in the field. We can move from a definition to a relationship, from a relationship to an Indian example, and from that example to a responsible intervention. The environment is not background scenery around human activity; it is the set of conditions that makes activity possible and sets its limits.

Case Study (India): Sundarbans Mangroves

Background: The Sundarbans are a large mangrove region in the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna delta, shared by India and Bangladesh. Tidal water, mudflats, channels and mangrove species create a highly productive but dynamic ecosystem.

What happened: Cyclones, salinity change, erosion, embankments, habitat pressure and climate-related sea-level risk have affected people and wildlife. The 2020 Amphan cyclone damaged homes, farms, embankments and vegetation across parts of the region.

Impact and response: Mangroves reduce wave energy and provide habitat for fish, birds and the Bengal tiger. Responses have included cyclone shelters, warning systems, embankment repair, mangrove restoration and protected-area management. Restoration is difficult where water flow and land use have changed.

Lesson: A mangrove is simultaneously habitat, nursery, carbon store and natural infrastructure. Protecting ecosystem function can reduce risk, but it must include the people living at the delta edge.

The EXTC Link

EXTC engineers can observe ecosystems without reducing them to one number. Camera traps use infrared triggers and timestamped images to estimate animal presence. Acoustic wildlife monitoring uses microphones to capture calls; band-pass filters remove irrelevant frequencies, and FFT-based features can support species classification. A Raspberry Pi can perform edge inference and send only detections, which saves bandwidth and avoids continuously uploading raw recordings.

For water ecosystems, temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, turbidity and water level need different sensors and maintenance plans. An ESP32 can schedule readings, while a solar-powered LoRaWAN node sends compact summaries. A flood-prone site may use a pressure transducer or ultrasonic level sensor, local threshold logic and a redundant cellular path. NB-IoT is useful where the operator network is stable; satellite or HF alternatives may be needed in remote disaster conditions.

Wildlife monitoring needs privacy and reliability. Device housings must withstand humidity, heat and insects. Time synchronisation matters when comparing multiple microphones. A calibration record should travel with each reading. If a camera trap misses an animal because its battery died, the absence is not evidence that the animal was absent. This is a signal-processing lesson and an ecological lesson at once.

We would build a system with three layers: edge sensing for immediate classification, a gateway for buffering and secure transport, and a dashboard that shows trends with confidence and missing-data flags. The design should support researchers and forest staff, not encourage intrusive monitoring. Technology helps ecosystems when it observes carefully and leaves room for ecological interpretation.

A low-bandwidth monitoring system can detect patterns while keeping raw media at the edge.

In practice, we would document assumptions, test failure modes and make the uncertainty visible to the person who has to act on the result. A maintainable prototype is more valuable than an impressive demonstration that cannot survive field conditions.

We would also plan ownership before deployment: who receives the alert, who can repair the node, who stores the data and who reviews a doubtful reading. A field protocol would include a reference measurement, a maintenance schedule and a way to report damage. These details connect embedded systems to environmental governance. Without them, a technically correct circuit can still produce an unreliable public service.

Finally, a deployment should have a human fallback. If the radio is unavailable, if the sensor drifts or if a model is uncertain, staff need a safe manual procedure. We would log these failures and use them to improve placement, thresholds and training. That feedback loop is the difference between a prototype and an accountable environmental system.
