Unit 05 · Source, pathway, receptor

Environmental Pollution

Pollution becomes a public-health issue when a harmful change reaches a person, organism or ecosystem.

On this pageSummaryVisualCase Study (India)The EXTC LinkAI-use note

Summary

Pollution is an undesirable change in air, water or land caused by substances, energy or waste at a level that harms life or use. The source may be a vehicle, factory, farm, sewage outlet, landfill, ship, construction site or electronic product. The pathway carries the pollutant through air, water, soil, food or sound. The receptor is the person, animal, plant, building or ecosystem affected. This source–pathway–receptor model helps us locate prevention instead of only describing symptoms.

Air pollution includes particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide, ozone and other pollutants. Health risk depends on concentration, duration, particle size and individual vulnerability. Water pollution may involve sewage, pathogens, nutrients, metals, oil, plastics and heat. Soil pollution can reduce fertility and move contaminants into crops or groundwater. Noise pollution is unwanted sound whose intensity, frequency and timing affect sleep, learning, wildlife and hearing.

Thermal pollution changes water temperature, often around power stations, and can reduce dissolved oxygen or stress aquatic organisms. Marine pollution includes plastics, oil, nutrients and chemicals carried from land or released at sea. Solid waste becomes dangerous when collection, segregation, treatment and disposal fail. E-waste contains recoverable materials but can also contain hazardous substances; informal dismantling exposes workers and releases contaminants. The E-Waste Management Rules 2022 establish extended producer responsibility mechanisms in India.

Control follows a hierarchy: avoid the pollutant, substitute a safer material, improve process control, capture emissions, treat waste, and monitor the result. CPCB and state pollution control boards operate regulatory and monitoring systems, including continuous air-quality stations. Disaster management adds preparedness, early warning, evacuation, emergency communication, recovery and learning. A warning that does not reach people or is not trusted is not a complete warning system.

Pollution is therefore an engineering and justice problem. Exposure is not evenly distributed, and a solution that moves contamination from air to sludge has not eliminated it. We need measurements with calibration, standards with enforcement, and designs that reduce waste before it is created.

That is why environmental study is also a habit of listening: measurements, local experience and institutional responsibility must inform one another before a decision is made.

Another useful distinction is between a problem, an indicator and an intervention. A falling water level is an indicator; the causes may include rainfall, pumping and land-use change; the intervention might be recharge, pricing, leakage control or a different crop. Asking these questions prevents us from treating a visible symptom as the whole environmental story. It also keeps public awareness practical, because people can see what information supports which choice.

This perspective is useful in an exam as well as in the field. We can move from a definition to a relationship, from a relationship to an Indian example, and from that example to a responsible intervention. The environment is not background scenery around human activity; it is the set of conditions that makes activity possible and sets its limits.

Case Study (India): Delhi Air Pollution

Background: Delhi and the National Capital Region experience severe air-pollution episodes, especially in winter. Sources vary by season and location and can include transport, industry, construction dust, household fuel, waste burning and regional agricultural fires.

What happened: High particulate concentrations and stagnant weather can combine to produce poor visibility and health advisories. The episode is not caused by one source everywhere, so simple blame can hide the need for coordinated action.

Impact and response: CPCB monitoring and the National Air Quality Index communicate conditions. Measures have included cleaner fuels, emission controls, public advisories, dust management, traffic actions and the Graded Response Action Plan. Results depend on enforcement, weather and regional cooperation.

Lesson: Public data should explain uncertainty and source context. Monitoring is valuable when it leads to cleaner transport, safer work conditions and timely protection for vulnerable people.

The EXTC Link

Air-quality engineering starts with the sensing layer. A PMS5003 can provide particulate readings for a low-cost prototype; an MQ-135 can respond to several gases but requires careful calibration and should not be presented as a CPCB-grade analyser. A DHT22 helps record humidity because particles and optical sensors can respond to moisture. A reference comparison, enclosure design, inlet protection and drift checks decide whether the result is useful.

An ESP32 can sample, timestamp and transmit data, while a Raspberry Pi gateway performs local aggregation. A Kalman or moving-median filter can reduce noise, but filtering must not hide short hazardous peaks. For industrial or official monitoring, certified analysers and proper quality assurance are required. A dashboard should show units, averaging period, missing data and a sensor-health state, not just a coloured badge.

Noise monitoring uses a calibrated microphone and a known sampling rate. FFT analysis shows frequency bands, helping distinguish traffic rumble from tonal machinery. Edge classification can detect persistent events without storing every audio sample. Water nodes use pH, TDS, turbidity and temperature sensors, with periodic grab samples for laboratory confirmation.

Smart waste bins can measure fill level using ultrasonic sensing and send a compact LoRaWAN packet to optimise collection. E-waste tracking can use QR or RFID labels, but the chain of custody still needs authorised recyclers and worker protections. Solar power and low-power scheduling reduce maintenance. During a chemical release or flood, 4G/5G, LoRaWAN and cell-broadcast alerts can provide complementary paths. The technical goal is a trusted, maintainable chain from pollutant signal to public action.

A monitoring system must expose sensor health and uncertainty before it exposes a warning.

In practice, we would document assumptions, test failure modes and make the uncertainty visible to the person who has to act on the result. A maintainable prototype is more valuable than an impressive demonstration that cannot survive field conditions.

We would also plan ownership before deployment: who receives the alert, who can repair the node, who stores the data and who reviews a doubtful reading. A field protocol would include a reference measurement, a maintenance schedule and a way to report damage. These details connect embedded systems to environmental governance. Without them, a technically correct circuit can still produce an unreliable public service.

Finally, a deployment should have a human fallback. If the radio is unavailable, if the sensor drifts or if a model is uncertain, staff need a safe manual procedure. We would log these failures and use them to improve placement, thresholds and training. That feedback loop is the difference between a prototype and an accountable environmental system.
