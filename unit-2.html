Unit 02 · Limits and choices

Natural Resources

Every resource has a source, a rate of renewal, a user and a conflict.

On this pageSummaryVisualCase Study (India)The EXTC LinkAI-use note

Summary

Natural resources are the materials and ecological services that support life and human activity. Forests provide timber, fuel, fodder, habitat, shade and soil protection. Water supports drinking, sanitation, irrigation, industry and ecosystems. Minerals become buildings, batteries, cables and electronics. Food resources depend on soil, water, seed diversity, pollinators and energy. Energy resources include coal, petroleum, gas, sunlight, wind, water and biomass. Land is the platform on which settlements, farms, transport and wildlife habitats compete.

We classify resources as renewable or non-renewable, but renewal is not the same as infinite supply. A groundwater aquifer may refill slowly while pumping happens daily. A forest can regrow in a human lifetime, yet a mature forest community and its soil may take much longer to recover. Fossil fuels form over geological time, so using them is effectively irreversible on our planning scale. The sensible question is not only “how much is there?” but also “at what rate can we use it without damaging the system that renews it?”

Over-use produces familiar patterns: deforestation, soil erosion, falling water tables, salinisation, mining damage, overfishing and the loss of grazing land. Resource conflicts arise because benefits and costs are distributed unevenly. A dam may provide electricity and irrigation while submerging villages. A mine may create jobs while changing water quality and land rights. Cities may draw water from distant rural watersheds. These are governance questions as much as technical ones.

Conservation means improving efficiency, reducing waste, restoring degraded land, harvesting rainwater, protecting recharge zones and choosing energy sources with lower life-cycle harm. It also means recognising common property and indigenous rights rather than assuming that a resource is free because it has no price. In India, watershed work, contour trenches, check dams and community forest management show how local action can change resource outcomes when institutions cooperate.

For an engineer, resource thinking appears in a product's entire life cycle. Copper, silicon, plastics and rare materials enter a device before it ever transmits a packet. A low-power design saves battery replacements; repairable hardware reduces mining and waste; a sensor network should not consume more energy than the environmental service it provides. Natural resources are the physical budget behind every technical system.

That is why environmental study is also a habit of listening: measurements, local experience and institutional responsibility must inform one another before a decision is made.

Another useful distinction is between a problem, an indicator and an intervention. A falling water level is an indicator; the causes may include rainfall, pumping and land-use change; the intervention might be recharge, pricing, leakage control or a different crop. Asking these questions prevents us from treating a visible symptom as the whole environmental story. It also keeps public awareness practical, because people can see what information supports which choice.

This perspective is useful in an exam as well as in the field. We can move from a definition to a relationship, from a relationship to an Indian example, and from that example to a responsible intervention. The environment is not background scenery around human activity; it is the set of conditions that makes activity possible and sets its limits.

Case Study (India): The Narmada Valley

Background: The Narmada basin supported villages, farms, forests and river ecosystems while large dams were proposed for irrigation, drinking water and electricity. The Sardar Sarovar project became a major national development project.

What happened: Reservoir filling and construction affected communities whose homes and agricultural land lay in the submergence area. The Narmada Bachao Andolan raised questions about displacement, rehabilitation, environmental assessment and who should define development.

Impact and response: The project went through long legal, political and institutional scrutiny. The World Bank withdrew from the project after an independent review raised serious concerns, while construction and rehabilitation continued under changing decisions and policies. The history is complex and affected people did not experience it uniformly.

Lesson: A resource project must count social and ecological costs before construction, publish credible impact information and provide fair rehabilitation. “More water” or “more power” is not a complete evaluation.

The EXTC Link

Resource monitoring is a distributed-systems problem. A soil-moisture probe can help schedule irrigation, a pH and TDS sensor can reveal changing water conditions, and a turbidity sensor can indicate sediment or runoff. An ESP32 node may read analogue probes through a stable reference and an external ADC; a Raspberry Pi gateway can aggregate readings, cache them and forward summaries. Sensor choice must follow the decision: a cheap conductivity reading is not a full water-quality assessment.

Calibration is central. Probes foul, electrodes age and temperature changes readings. We would take field blanks, compare periodic samples with a laboratory method, record calibration coefficients and expose a confidence flag in the dashboard. A moving average can smooth irrigation data, while a median filter is safer for occasional spikes. Sampling frequency should match the process: groundwater changes slowly, a pump cycle quickly, and a flood can change within minutes.

LoRaWAN is efficient for farm nodes sending small packets to a gateway, while NB-IoT or 4G works when an operator network is available. Zigbee can connect devices within a greenhouse. Solar charging with deep sleep extends field life, but the energy budget must include cold starts, sensor warm-up and retransmissions. Store-and-forward makes a node useful when a valley has intermittent coverage.

At system level, remote sensing can estimate land cover and water spread over large areas. ISRO platforms and Bhuvan provide Indian geospatial context, but satellite data needs ground truth. The engineering output is therefore a calibrated map and a measured uncertainty, not a colourful layer. Resource decisions become better when field sensing, satellite observation and community knowledge are brought together.

Proposed watershed loop: measure, validate, communicate and adjust use.

In practice, we would document assumptions, test failure modes and make the uncertainty visible to the person who has to act on the result. A maintainable prototype is more valuable than an impressive demonstration that cannot survive field conditions.

We would also plan ownership before deployment: who receives the alert, who can repair the node, who stores the data and who reviews a doubtful reading. A field protocol would include a reference measurement, a maintenance schedule and a way to report damage. These details connect embedded systems to environmental governance. Without them, a technically correct circuit can still produce an unreliable public service.

Finally, a deployment should have a human fallback. If the radio is unavailable, if the sensor drifts or if a model is uncertain, staff need a safe manual procedure. We would log these failures and use them to improve placement, thresholds and training. That feedback loop is the difference between a prototype and an accountable environmental system.
