The Multidisciplinary Nature of Environmental Studies

Environment is the meeting point of natural science, engineering, economics, law and everyday choices.

On this pageSummaryVisualCase Study (India)The EXTC LinkAI-use note

Summary

Environmental studies asks how living organisms, physical surroundings and human decisions affect one another. Its scope is wider than a single subject because a real environmental problem does not arrive divided into exam chapters. A polluted river involves chemistry, microbiology, hydrology, public health, farming, economics, law and the behaviour of the people using that river. We need all of those viewpoints to understand both the damage and a workable response.

The natural sciences help us observe the system. Ecology explains relationships among organisms, geology explains soil and minerals, and atmospheric science explains weather and climate. Engineering then turns knowledge into action through treatment plants, safer materials, monitoring networks and resilient infrastructure. Social science asks who receives benefits, who bears risk and whether a proposed solution is trusted. Environmental law creates duties and limits, while economics helps compare costs without pretending that every loss can be reduced to money.

Studying the environment matters because our health, food, water, energy and livelihoods depend on ecological functions that are easy to notice only after they fail. A wetland may look like unused land until it stores stormwater. A tree may look like a roadside obstacle until we measure shade, dust capture and cooling. Environmental education gives us the habit of asking what is connected, what is changing, and over what time scale.

There is also a practical need for public awareness. Laws and technologies work poorly when people do not know how waste is separated, why a local water body matters, or how to respond to a heat wave. Awareness is not just a poster campaign. It means clear information, participation in decisions, access to grievance systems and the ability to distinguish evidence from rumours. In India, local knowledge from farmers, fishers and forest communities often adds observations that a short survey misses.

For us as EXTC students, the unit changes the idea of a device. A sensor is part of a social and ecological system: someone powers it, someone interprets its reading, and someone acts on an alert. The best environmental work therefore combines measurement with humility. We cannot solve a watershed from a dashboard alone, just as a circuit diagram cannot explain who gets connected to a network. Environmental studies gives us the vocabulary to design responsibly.

That is why environmental study is also a habit of listening: measurements, local experience and institutional responsibility must inform one another before a decision is made.

Another useful distinction is between a problem, an indicator and an intervention. A falling water level is an indicator; the causes may include rainfall, pumping and land-use change; the intervention might be recharge, pricing, leakage control or a different crop. Asking these questions prevents us from treating a visible symptom as the whole environmental story. It also keeps public awareness practical, because people can see what information supports which choice.

This perspective is useful in an exam as well as in the field. We can move from a definition to a relationship, from a relationship to an Indian example, and from that example to a responsible intervention. The environment is not background scenery around human activity; it is the set of conditions that makes activity possible and sets its limits.

Case Study (India): The Chipko Movement

Background: In the 1970s, villagers in the Himalayan region of present-day Uttarakhand depended on forests for fuel, fodder, water security and soil stability. Commercial forest use did not always reflect those local needs.

What happened: In 1973, villagers in the Chamoli area resisted tree felling by physically embracing trees. Women were prominent participants, and the movement became known as Chipko. It was a form of non-violent environmental action as well as a demand for fair access to forest resources.

Impact and response: The movement drew national attention to deforestation, landslides, local livelihoods and the value of community participation. Government policy later placed restrictions on some Himalayan tree felling, although forest governance has continued to involve debate and change.

Lesson: A forest is not merely timber inventory. Good environmental decisions combine ecological measurements with the experience of people who depend on the landscape. Public awareness becomes powerful when it is connected to voice and responsibility.

The EXTC Link

Our engineering contribution to multidisciplinary environmental work begins with a measurement chain: phenomenon → sensor → signal conditioning → edge device → network → decision. For air quality, a PMS5003 can estimate particulate concentrations while an MQ-135 can provide a broad gas-response signal. Neither should be treated as a laboratory instrument by default. We would calibrate against a reference station where possible, record temperature and humidity using a DHT22, and store sensor identity, location and timestamp with every reading.

An ESP32 is useful at the edge because it combines analogue and digital interfaces, sleep modes and Wi-Fi/Bluetooth. A remote forest or watershed node may instead use a low-power microcontroller with a LoRaWAN radio. LoRaWAN can carry small packets over long distances with low energy, while NB-IoT or 4G/5G is more suitable where operator coverage and two-way service are available. Zigbee fits a short-range mesh around a campus. Satellite links matter when a disaster damages terrestrial networks or when a monitoring site is genuinely isolated.

Signal processing makes the data less fragile. We can reject impossible values, apply a moving median to remove spikes, compensate for humidity where the sensor model supports it, and flag slow calibration drift. For acoustic monitoring, sampling rate must cover the frequencies of interest; an FFT can turn a time waveform into a frequency view and identify persistent machine noise. Edge ML can classify a sound or detect an unusual pattern without sending raw audio, reducing bandwidth and privacy risk.

Power is an environmental design constraint. A small solar panel, charge controller and battery need an energy budget that includes radio transmit peaks, not just average current. Duty cycling, local buffering and deep sleep often matter more than a faster processor. The proposed system is useful only if a local institution can maintain it and understand its uncertainty. That is the multidisciplinary lesson in hardware form.

Proposed community monitoring chain: the output is a decision, not just a number.

In practice, we would document assumptions, test failure modes and make the uncertainty visible to the person who has to act on the result. A maintainable prototype is more valuable than an impressive demonstration that cannot survive field conditions.

We would also plan ownership before deployment: who receives the alert, who can repair the node, who stores the data and who reviews a doubtful reading. A field protocol would include a reference measurement, a maintenance schedule and a way to report damage. These details connect embedded systems to environmental governance. Without them, a technically correct circuit can still produce an unreliable public service.

Finally, a deployment should have a human fallback. If the radio is unavailable, if the sensor drifts or if a model is uncertain, staff need a safe manual procedure. We would log these failures and use them to improve placement, thresholds and training. That feedback loop is the difference between a prototype and an accountable environmental system.
