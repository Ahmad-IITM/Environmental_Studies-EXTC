Unit 06 · Futures and fairness

Social Issues and Environment

Sustainability asks whether a solution can last, scale fairly and leave room for the next generation.

On this pageSummaryVisualCase Study (India)The EXTC LinkAI-use note

Summary

Sustainable development means meeting present needs while protecting the ability of future generations to meet theirs. It is not a promise to stop development. It asks us to balance ecological limits, social equity and economic security. Water conservation shows the idea clearly: rainwater harvesting, recharge, efficient irrigation, leak reduction and wastewater reuse can support people while protecting a watershed. A solution is stronger when users can afford it, maintain it and influence how it is governed.

Climate change describes long-term changes in temperature and weather patterns. Human emissions of greenhouse gases increase radiative forcing, contributing to global warming and changes in rainfall, heat waves, sea level and extreme-event risk. Adaptation reduces harm through heat action plans, resilient infrastructure, crop choices, mangrove protection and early warning. Mitigation reduces emissions through efficiency, renewable energy, public transport, cleaner industry and protection of carbon-rich ecosystems.

Acid rain forms when sulphur and nitrogen compounds react in the atmosphere and return with precipitation or dry deposition. Ozone depletion concerns chemicals that destroy stratospheric ozone, which shields life from ultraviolet radiation; it is different from ground-level ozone pollution. The Montreal Protocol shows that international cooperation can reduce a global environmental threat, while climate action remains a much broader transition.

Development can also create displacement. Resettlement and rehabilitation must address land, housing, livelihoods, social networks and consent rather than treating payment as the whole answer. Indian environmental governance includes the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974, Air Act 1981, Environment (Protection) Act 1986, Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972 and newer rules such as the E-Waste Management Rules 2022. Laws matter through institutions, monitoring, participation and enforcement.

Consumerism increases extraction and waste when products are designed for short use or replacement. The practical response is sufficiency, repair, reuse, producer responsibility, segregation and circular design. As engineers, we should ask how long a device lasts, whether its battery and parts can be replaced, how much energy its network consumes, and what happens after collection. Sustainability is not a decorative label; it is a design requirement and a question of justice.

That is why environmental study is also a habit of listening: measurements, local experience and institutional responsibility must inform one another before a decision is made.

Another useful distinction is between a problem, an indicator and an intervention. A falling water level is an indicator; the causes may include rainfall, pumping and land-use change; the intervention might be recharge, pricing, leakage control or a different crop. Asking these questions prevents us from treating a visible symptom as the whole environmental story. It also keeps public awareness practical, because people can see what information supports which choice.

This perspective is useful in an exam as well as in the field. We can move from a definition to a relationship, from a relationship to an Indian example, and from that example to a responsible intervention. The environment is not background scenery around human activity; it is the set of conditions that makes activity possible and sets its limits.

Case Study (India): Kerala Floods, 2018

Background: Kerala has dense settlements, steep terrain in parts of the Western Ghats, rivers, backwaters and a monsoon-dependent economy. In 2018 the state experienced exceptionally severe flooding and landslides after intense rainfall over a period of days.

What happened: Floodwater and landslides affected homes, roads, electricity, communications, farms and public services. Reservoir operations, rainfall, catchment condition and settlement patterns all formed part of a complex disaster context; no single explanation covers every location.

Impact and response: State agencies, national forces, local governments, fishers and volunteers supported rescue, relief and recovery. Warnings, evacuation, shelters and restoration became central. Later discussions emphasised resilient land-use planning, catchment management, hazard maps and communication redundancy.

Lesson: Disaster management is a cycle, not a siren: risk reduction, preparedness, response, recovery and learning must connect. Reliable communications save lives only when messages are timely, local and actionable.

The EXTC Link

Climate adaptation needs networks that keep working under stress. A flood node can combine ultrasonic or pressure water-level sensing, rainfall measurement and a local threshold algorithm. An ESP32 can wake, sample and transmit over LoRaWAN; a gateway can forward data over 4G/5G. If the cellular path fails, the node can store readings and a second gateway or satellite terminal can carry priority alerts. Battery and solar sizing must include monsoon cloud cover, corrosion and maintenance access.

Forecast and warning systems combine radar, satellite observations, gauges and numerical models. ISRO remote sensing and Bhuvan mapping can provide spatial context, while ground stations validate water extent. Signal processing removes sensor spikes and estimates a trend, but a warning policy should define thresholds with disaster managers. Cell-broadcast alerts are powerful because they can reach compatible phones in an area without requiring a contact list; messages still need local language, accessibility and trusted instructions.

Green computing applies to telecom equipment too. Sleep modes, efficient modulation, edge aggregation and renewable backup reduce energy use. A network planner can compare coverage, latency, reliability and embodied hardware rather than maximising data rate alone. NB-IoT suits small periodic packets, while 4G/5G supports richer data and low-latency coordination. Zigbee can connect sensors inside a shelter or campus microgrid.

Climate systems also need social telemetry: who received the message, who could evacuate, which roads were usable and which groups were missed. Privacy, cybersecurity and open standards protect trust. The engineering deliverable is a resilient service with clear failure modes, not a glossy dashboard. In environmental work, communication is part of the infrastructure that communities depend on.

Proposed warning chain with local decisions and multiple communication paths.

In practice, we would document assumptions, test failure modes and make the uncertainty visible to the person who has to act on the result. A maintainable prototype is more valuable than an impressive demonstration that cannot survive field conditions.

We would also plan ownership before deployment: who receives the alert, who can repair the node, who stores the data and who reviews a doubtful reading. A field protocol would include a reference measurement, a maintenance schedule and a way to report damage. These details connect embedded systems to environmental governance. Without them, a technically correct circuit can still produce an unreliable public service.

Finally, a deployment should have a human fallback. If the radio is unavailable, if the sensor drifts or if a model is uncertain, staff need a safe manual procedure. We would log these failures and use them to improve placement, thresholds and training. That feedback loop is the difference between a prototype and an accountable environmental system.
